About C*Power v0.1

What is C*Power?

C*Power is a browser-based, mobile-friendly clone of G*Power, generated by a large language model (Claude by Anthropic). It requires no installation and runs entirely in your browser — including on smartphones and tablets.

⚠️ WORK IN PROGRESS: Calculations in this tool have NOT been independently validated against G*Power or other statistical software. Results may contain errors. Do not rely on this tool for published research without cross-checking against a validated tool.

References cited in the parameter tooltips and method descriptions

The effect size benchmarks, conventions, and test formulas shown when you hover the ⓘ icons or open the "R Code" panel are drawn from:

  • Cohen, J. (1988). Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences (2nd ed.). Hillsdale, NJ: Lawrence Erlbaum. — The classical reference for "small / medium / large" conventions for d, f, w, h, f², r, and for most of the effect-size formulas used throughout this tool.
  • Fisher, R. A. (1921). On the probable error of a coefficient of correlation deduced from a small sample. Metron, 1, 3–32. — Origin of the z-transformation used for the one-sample correlation test.
  • Fisher, R. A. (1925). Statistical Methods for Research Workers. Edinburgh: Oliver & Boyd. — Basis for the test comparing two independent correlation coefficients.
  • Steiger, J. H. (1980). Tests for comparing elements of a correlation matrix. Psychological Bulletin, 87(2), 245–251. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.87.2.245 — Formula used for comparing two dependent (overlapping) correlations.
  • Dunn, O. J., & Clark, V. (1969). Correlation coefficients measured on the same individuals. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 64(325), 366–377. — Earlier treatment of dependent-correlation comparisons, refined by Steiger (1980).
  • Gignac, G. E., & Szodorai, E. T. (2016). Effect size guidelines for individual differences researchers. Personality and Individual Differences, 102, 74–78. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2016.06.069 — Empirical distribution of effect sizes (median r ≈ 0.19, median d ≈ 0.36).
  • Lakens, D. (2013). Calculating and reporting effect sizes to facilitate cumulative science: A practical primer for t-tests and ANOVAs. Frontiers in Psychology, 4, 863. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00863 — Practical guide to Cohen's d, dz, d_av and η²/ω².
  • Lakens, D., & Evers, E. R. K. (2014). Sailing from the seas of chaos into the corridor of stability: Practical recommendations to increase the informational value of studies. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 9(3), 278–292. — Recommends targeting power ≥ 0.90.
  • Hattie, J. (2009). Visible Learning: A synthesis of over 800 meta-analyses relating to achievement. London: Routledge. — Identifies d ≈ 0.40 as the educational "hinge point".
  • Ioannidis, J. P. A. (2005). Why most published research findings are false. PLoS Medicine, 2(8), e124. — On false discovery rates & the cost of low power.
  • Benjamin, D. J., et al. (2018). Redefine statistical significance. Nature Human Behaviour, 2, 6–10. — Proposed lowering α to 0.005 for new findings.
  • Lorah, J. (2018). Effect size measures for multilevel models: definition, interpretation, and TIMSS example. Large-scale Assessments in Education, 6, 8. — Domain-specific effect size benchmarks beyond Cohen.
  • Cumming, G. (2014). The new statistics: Why and how. Psychological Science, 25(1), 7–29. — On reporting effect sizes and confidence intervals.
  • Maxwell, S. E., & Delaney, H. D. (2004). Designing Experiments and Analyzing Data (2nd ed.). Mahwah, NJ: Erlbaum. — Repeated-measures ANOVA power formulas.

R packages referenced in the exportable R code

The "R Code" panel (available next to each result) generates code using:

  • pwr (Champely, S., 2020) — used for t-tests, ANOVA, chi-square, regression f², and proportions, wherever a direct function exists.
  • cocor (Diedenhofen & Musch, 2015) — referenced for independently cross-checking the two correlation-comparison tests (does not itself perform power analysis, but its significance-test statistics were used to validate C*Power's noncentrality formulas).
  • Base R noncentral distribution functions (qf/pf, qt/pt, qchisq/pchisq with the ncp= argument) — used wherever no dedicated pwr function exists (ANCOVA, repeated measures, MANOVA, dependent correlations), replicating exactly the same computation C*Power performs in JavaScript.

Statistical methods used

  • Normal CDF: Abramowitz & Stegun (1964) rational approximation; inverse via Beasley-Springer-Moro algorithm.
  • Central t, F, χ² CDFs: regularised incomplete beta and gamma functions (Lentz continued fraction; Pike-Hill symmetry for numerical stability at extreme parameter values).
  • Noncentral F: exact Poisson-weighted mixture of regularised incomplete-beta functions — P(F′≤f) = Σⱼ Pois(j;λ/2)·I_y(df₁/2+j, df₂/2), validated against R's pf(...,ncp=).
  • Noncentral t: exact via the relationship |t′|² ~ F′(1, df, ncp²) for two-tailed tests; normal approximation for df ≥ 30 (indistinguishable from exact at that range).
  • Noncentral χ²: exact Poisson-weighted mixture of central χ² distributions, validated against R's pchisq(...,ncp=).
  • Correlation tests: one-sample test replicates R's pwr.r.test exactly (validated to 4 decimal places); two independent correlations replicates Fisher (1925), validated against cocor::cocor.indep.groups; two dependent correlations replicates Steiger (1980), validated against cocor::cocor.dep.groups.overlap.
  • Sample-size search: stepwise integer search (F/χ² family) or bisection (t/z/correlation family) starting from the minimum valid N (df ≥ 1).
  • Sensitivity / criterion analyses: bisection on the bracketed, monotone power function.
